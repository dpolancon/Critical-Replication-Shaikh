# 2 A Historical Trace of Capacity Utilization Measurement

This section reconstructs capacity-utilization measurement as a historical problem of statecraft and identification. Capacity utilization first became legible through survey-based measurement practices tied to demand management, then was recoded into output-gap governance as macroeconomic institutions required portable benchmarks for slack. Political economy later reopened the question, but did so through inherited measurement instruments whose construction limited what they could prove. The section follows this historical trace across four steps: the emergence of survey-based capacity-utilization measurement as a practice of statecraft, its recoding into output-gap governance, the reopening of capacity utilization within political economy, and Shaikh’s route from proxy measurement toward structural identification through profitability accounting.

## 2.1 From Survey Measures to an Inflation Sentinel: Capacity Utilization Measurements during the Fordist Era

The Fordist history of capacity-utilization measurement begins with the problem of making idle productive capacity visible, rather than with a settled variable. The Great Depression made idle resources visible as a political and economic problem, but not yet as a stabilized object of macroeconomic management. Brookings’ research was decisive because it turned unused productive capacity from an impressionistic sign of downturn into a measurable ceiling, forcing institutions to decide what counted as capacity, how it could be approximated, and how that approximation could guide macroeconomic intervention. Later assessments made clear that this early construction rested on unresolved tensions between engineering and economic notions of capacity (Burns, 1954; Klein & David, 1958). The early history of utilization measurement was therefore already a history of statecraft through numbers rather than a neutral exercise in statistical description.

American Keynesianism gave that problem a clearer institutional and theoretical function. Hansen’s interwar and wartime thesis on secular stagnation explained why productive slack could no longer remain secondary once stabilization became a policy objective (Hansen, 1936, 1941; Barber, 1987). Hicks and Samuelson then translated that problem into a formal and pedagogical grammar, turning slack into a governable macroeconomic object through the IS–LM framework that shaped graduate training in the United States (Hicks, 1937; Samuelson, 1947, 1948). The Keynesian State constituted itself by managing demand, sustaining employment, and monitoring bottlenecks. Hence, it required indicators that translated underutilized productive capacities into signals for fiscal and monetary intervention. Capacity utilization became necessary not because Keynesianism discovered a timeless magnitude, but because a policy regime organized around full employment required an index through which effective-demand deficiencies could be rendered legible to the authorities charged with stabilization.

Theoretical consolidation did not by itself settle the operational routine. McGraw–Hill Publishing Company did much of that work. Its plant-and-equipment surveys regularized managerial reports of actual and preferred operating rates and, in doing so, converted heterogeneous notions of productive capacity into a repeated business-information device (Butler, 1958; Phillips, 1963). What mattered was less the resolution of conceptual ambiguity than the creation of an institutionally reproducible benchmark. Under Fordism, reproducibility itself became a source of authority: repeated survey collection generated comparability across time and sectors, while the broader information infrastructure of monopoly capital enhanced the measurement’s legitimacy inside business forecasting and policy discussion (Baran & Sweezy, 1988; Munroe, 2007). What emerged was not a better estimate of productive ceilings, but an operational routine through which firms, analysts, and policymakers could speak a common language about slack.

The Federal Reserve then transformed that routine into official macroeconomic authority (Board of Governors of the Federal Reserve System, 2017). Once capacity and utilization measures were incorporated into the Industrial Production program, the survey benchmark ceased to be one possible way of gauging slack and became an official source for macroeconomic decision-making. Institutional embedding did not simply add prestige to an existing measure; it raised the switching costs of alternatives and standardized what counted as admissible evidence. Physical-intensity measures such as Electric Motors Utilization and the Average Workweek of Capital remained conceptually important and in some respects tracked operational realities more directly, but failed to secure comparable institutional adoption (Foss, 1963; Taubman & Gottschalk, 1971). Not every rival was empirically defeated: one routine proved easier to reproduce administratively, became embedded in bureaucratic practice, and entered the state’s statistical machinery.

The crisis of the 1970s did not abolish that routine. It triggered its recodification and opened the path toward new operational routines. Under stagflationary pressures, capacity utilization was detached from its role as a signal of effective-demand slack and reinterpreted as an inflation sentinel. The benchmark survived, but its admissible meaning narrowed: what had once justified expansionary intervention now mattered insofar as it signaled when effective output was generating inflationary pressure. This appears clearly in the Brookings debates, where capacity measures were increasingly judged by whether they could deliver credible accounts of inflation (Klein et al., 1973). The shift was not a minor technical refinement but a crisis-driven transformation of the policy dashboard itself. As the Bretton Woods order unraveled, the Fordist compromise weakened, and profitability pressures intensified, the same operational routine that had once organized full-employment management was made compatible with emerging anti-inflation discipline. Its durability across this transition did not resolve the measurement problem. It prepared the ground for a new measurement framework in which slack would be governed less through survey-centered utilization series than through output-gap benchmarks that could travel across supranational institutions and later be reterritorialized through national states.

## 2.2 From Capacity Utilization to Output-Gap Governance

The transition from survey-centered capacity utilization to output-gap governance was not simply a technical change in measurement. It was a change in the operational routine through which macroeconomic institutions made slack legible and actionable. The Fordist survey routine had been nationally embedded, sectorally anchored, and tied to the policy world of demand management. Post-Fordist macroeconomic governance required a more portable benchmark: one that could travel across central banks, fiscal authorities, budget offices, and supranational surveillance institutions.

Johnson’s (2024) reconstruction of the Lucas critique clarifies the intellectual form of this transition. The issue was not whether the state would act, but what kind of state action could count as scientific. Discretionary intervention became suspect because it changed the policy environment faced by agents whose expectations econometric models required to be stable. Rule-bound governance, by contrast, promised a form of state action that could operate through credibility, pre-announced commitments, and expectation management. Output-gap estimation became a central operational routine through which institutions sought to meet that demand: it translated slack into a benchmark legible to rule-based policy frameworks, inflation monitoring, and fiscal surveillance.

Post-Fordism therefore names a reorganization of statecraft, not a mere period label. Under conditions of spatio-temporal compression—the acceleration of capital circulation, the re-scaling of state authority toward supranational institutions, and the growing authority of portable methods legible within neoclassical economics—macroeconomic measurement had to operate across borders and policy scales (Jessop, 2002, 2003). The demise of the Bretton Woods financial architecture cannot be reduced to free capital mobility alone. It also carried the circulation of methods aligned with the interests of finance as the hegemonic fraction of an emergent transnational capitalist class, whose discourse of globalization was mediated by bureaucratic and technical fractions (Jessop, 2002, 2003; Sklair, 2000).

The material infrastructure of this transformation mattered. Faster shipping times, the reorganization of production around global value chains and a new international division of labour, and the accelerated development of data processing and telecommunications created a historically specific governance demand (Fröbel et al., 1978; Jessop, 2003). The Society for Worldwide Interbank Financial Telecommunication, established in 1973, marks this infrastructure with particular clarity. Inherited Fordist routines could not travel easily across the jurisdictions and scales that globalized accumulation required (Jessop, 2003). What emerged was a post-Fordist tendency in macroeconomic governance: the displacement of real-time discretionary demand management by rule-based and pre-announced frameworks whose authority rested not on the correct tracking of business cycles, but on the institutional consensus that depoliticized technocratic routines could substitute for discretionary judgment (Saad-Filho, 2018).

This tendency recoded the benchmark itself. In place of capacity utilization as a slack measure, the output gap became the central concept for distinguishing cyclical from trend behavior in mainstream macroeconomics. Within mainstream macroeconomics, the transformation was also disciplinary: the language of capacity utilization was increasingly recoded through econometric routines for estimating potential output. Output-gap estimation collapsed the complexity of capacity identification into a portable governance variable, one benchmark that could be read simultaneously by surveillance bodies, budget offices, and central banks. But this historical development did not resolve the contradictions of governance under globalized accumulation. It displaced them into a portable benchmark whose closure assumptions were rarely made explicit, until the Global Financial Crisis of 2008 placed the output-gap apparatus under pressures it was not designed to absorb (Jessop, 2003).

The decisive critical juncture through which this tendency consolidated was the Volcker Shock. It was not simply a victory of anti-inflation orthodoxy over Keynesian demand management. Its institutional groundwork had been developing for at least two decades. From the 1960s onward, Eurodollar lending, offshore credit intermediation, and the progressive erosion of Bretton Woods capital controls had already begun to rework the structural foundations of U.S. financial power (Konings, 2007). The monetarist turn of the late 1970s should therefore not be read as a retreat under external market pressure, but as a reconfiguration of the balance of forces led by financial capital to reshape the international monetary order in its own image. By the time Volcker raised rates, accumulation had already been shifting from commodity production toward financial channels (Krippner, 2005). The shock did not initiate that shift. It consolidated the institutional conditions under which financial accumulation could become hegemonic.

What elite narratives later presented as technical correction was in practice a political settlement with distributional consequences (Morgan & Sheehan, 2015). The credibility established through the Volcker intervention had to be reproduced through new operational routines. Central bank transparency—the public communication of targets, forecasts, and decision rationales—was constructed during this period not as neutral modernization but as a mechanism for managing expectations instead of demand (Krippner, 2007). Transparency replaced discretion as the operating principle and helped institutionalize the transition from a regime that acted on the economy to one that governed through the credibility of its own announced commitments.

The rational-expectations revolution gave that transition its theoretical form. It was anti-judgment rather than anti-state: what had to be eliminated was not state action, but the ability of authorities to change course in ways agents could not anticipate (Johnson, 2024). The Volcker Shock itself was not rule-based. It was a discretionary act of extraordinary severity whose function was to establish the credibility that formal rules would later require. What the 1980s achieved through demonstrated willingness to impose costs, the 1990s formalized as portable architecture: the Taylor rule, inflation targeting, and central bank independence converted hard-won credibility into a framework defensible as technically neutral (Johnson, 2024; Taylor, 1993).

Saad-Filho (2018) names this settlement the New Monetary Policy Consensus: a regime whose coherence rests on foreclosing the forms of contestation that demand management had left open. Distributional conflict did not disappear. It was recoded as a threat to credibility. The structural favorability of low inflation to financial capital, the narrowing of fiscal space, and the depoliticization of employment outcomes were rendered invisible because the framework presented its own institutional architecture as the only arrangement consistent with macroeconomic stability. In Latin America, where structural adjustment programmes compressed the wage share of national income by an average of eight percentage points during the 1980s and 1990s—and by 24.4 percent in Chile and 33.8 percent in Ecuador—these consequences were anything but invisible to the populations that bore them (Hickel et al., 2026).

The New Monetary Policy Consensus did not consolidate through central banks alone. It sedimented across supranational and national institutions, each embedding the output gap as a governance variable inside its own operational mandate. But the output gap is not directly observable; it is produced through an identification problem. Actual output is observed, while potential output must be estimated and expressed relative to that benchmark. Potential output must itself be produced through maintained assumptions about the structure of the economy. Every output-gap estimate therefore carries a closure: a set of identifying restrictions that determines what counts as potential output and how far actual output can be judged to deviate from it.

Three broad families of estimation dominated post-Fordist practice. Each supplied a different closure for constructing the unobserved benchmark. The first is statistical filtering: decomposing an observed output series into trend and cycle through mechanical smoothing. The Hodrick–Prescott filter extracts a slow-moving trend by penalizing its second differences against the fit to data, with a smoothing parameter chosen by convention rather than estimated (Hodrick & Prescott, 1997). Band-pass filters isolate cyclical fluctuations within a pre-specified periodicity window (Baxter & King, 1999; Christiano & Fitzgerald, 2003). The Beveridge and Nelson decomposition identifies the permanent component of output as the long-horizon forecast of a fitted ARIMA process, so that the cycle is the transitory deviation from the stochastic trend (Beveridge & Nelson, 1981). Hamilton argues that the Hodrick–Prescott filter can generate spurious cyclical dynamics in integrated series and proposes a regression-based alternative, though the later literature remains divided (Franke et al., 2024; Hamilton, 2018; Siemers, 2025). What unites these methods is that potential output is constructed from the statistical properties of the series itself. Their closure is statistical: the trend becomes potential output by construction, and the gap inherits whatever cyclical behavior the smoothing procedure imposes.

The second family imposes economic structure directly through aggregate production functions. The standard implementation—used by the Congressional Budget Office for the United States and adapted by the Directorate-General for Economic and Financial Affairs of the European Commission—specifies a Cobb–Douglas production function in which potential output is determined by trend total factor productivity, the capital stock, and potential labor input derived from an estimated non-accelerating inflation rate of unemployment (Congressional Budget Office, 2004; Havik et al., 2014). The closure here is neoclassical in the precise sense: factor markets clear at structurally given rates, and the gap measures the deviation of actual output from the level consistent with those equilibrium factor inputs. In International Monetary Fund research, early systems approaches that estimated potential output and the natural rate jointly later evolved into multivariate filters that combine statistical decomposition with economic priors, typically through a New Keynesian Phillips-curve relation (Adams & Coe, 1990; Alichi, 2015; Barkema et al., 2020). These methods occupy an intermediate position: they do not impose a full structural model, but they anchor the statistical object through selected economic relationships.

The third family identifies potential output through structural vector autoregressions. The canonical Blanchard–Quah decomposition imposes a long-run restriction: demand shocks have no permanent effect on output, allowing permanent movements to be attributed to supply shocks and transitory deviations to demand shocks (Blanchard & Quah, 1989). This is the most theoretically explicit closure of the three families because the output gap inherits a causal claim about the economy’s structure rather than a purely statistical convention or a maintained production function. It is also the most vulnerable to its premise. If demand shocks have persistent or permanent effects—through hysteresis, endogenous capacity destruction, or path-dependent investment—the restriction fails, and the decomposition attributes to supply what was in fact demand-driven structural change.

Output-gap estimation became durable not because any one method proved superior, but because the procedure sedimented across institutions that conduct macroeconomic surveillance, administer fiscal rules, and structure programme conditionality. At the International Monetary Fund, the gap estimate enters assessments of fiscal stance, adjustment needs, and timelines, making it a load-bearing variable in fiscal sustainability analysis and conditionality. Structural adjustment programmes built around these assessments have been associated with reduced government health expenditure, rising income inequality, and concentration of income gains into the top decile (Kentikelenis & Stubbs, 2023, pp. 93, 123, 131). At the Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development, the same object appears as a medium-term, inflation-consistent benchmark embedded in Economic Outlook projections, country surveys, and structural-balance calculations (Torres & Martin, 1990). At the Congressional Budget Office, potential output is tied directly to the budget baseline: the gap estimate helps determine what counts as the cyclically adjusted budget balance (Congressional Budget Office, 2004). At the Directorate-General for Economic and Financial Affairs of the European Commission, production-function estimates anchored by the non-accelerating wage rate of unemployment feed into the Stability and Growth Pact, the European Semester, and structural-balance calculations, adjudicating fiscal space, discipline, and excess (Havik et al., 2014). The same benchmark family operates as assessment in one institutional setting and enforcement in another. That functional versatility, grounded in closure assumptions rarely surfaced in policy discourse, is what makes the apparatus durable.

The Global Financial Crisis did not dislodge this settlement. It exposed the conditions on which its durability had depended: moderate shocks, anchored expectations, and institutional environments in which measurement error could be absorbed without triggering the political consequences that depoliticization was designed to foreclose (Best, 2025). The crisis made visible what the apparatus had structurally concealed: output-gap estimates are revision-prone, closure-dependent, and unreliable in real time precisely when institutional demand for them is greatest. Post-crisis revisions were large enough to change the sign of the gap and, with it, the policy signal the benchmark was supposed to deliver (Kavřík, 2026). In the European periphery, the consequences were not only epistemic but fiscal: structural-balance calculations built on biased potential-output estimates compressed public investment during the deepest demand shortfalls in postwar history (Heimberger & Kapeller, 2017; Heimberger, 2020). Nor did the post-pandemic recovery break the pattern: fifteen of twenty-one programmes signed between 2020 and 2022 featured decreased fiscal space, and social spending floors were implemented at a lower rate than the austerity conditions they were meant to offset (Kentikelenis & Stubbs, 2023, pp. 178–183).

Two tendencies became visible after the crisis. The first was self-reflexive correction within the research bodies of the institutions that produce the estimates. Cerra and Saxena (2017) argued that deep recessions are not transitory deviations from a stable trend but events with permanent effects on the level of output, directly challenging the identification assumptions that underwrite filter-based and production-function estimates of potential output. Cerra et al. (2023) extended that diagnosis into a systematic account of hysteresis, documenting mechanisms through which demand shocks leave permanent traces in output, employment, and productivity. Within the International Monetary Fund, this line of work translated into revision audits, uncertainty quantification, and recognition that real-time output-gap estimates can carry measurement error large enough to reverse the policy signals derived from them (Aiyar & Voigts, 2024; Barkema et al., 2020). This is real institutional learning, but it operates within the apparatus rather than displacing its closure. Estimates are recalibrated, hedged, and qualified; the procedure itself remains.

The second tendency ran in the opposite direction: deeper real-time integration of output-gap estimates into monetary-policy operations. Nowcasting models, dynamic factor methods, and forward-guidance architectures made the gap more central to policy, not less (Aastveit & Trovik, 2014; Barbarino et al., 2020; Berger et al., 2023; Grigoli et al., 2015). The two tendencies coexist as a structural contradiction: research arms document the fragility of the benchmark while operational arms deepen its role.

The contradiction runs deeper than a mismatch between knowledge and practice. Foster (2024) traces how the ideological foundations of central bank technocracy were reworked after 2008 not by abandoning neutral expertise, but by stretching it to accommodate mandates the original framework was not designed to carry. The European Central Bank’s 2021 strategy revision is the clearest institutional expression: broadening the mandate, incorporating climate and distributional considerations, and reinterpreting the framework in terms that Argyroulis and Vagdoutis characterize as “broad and vague rather than clear and narrow” (Argyroulis & Vagdoutis, 2025, p. 14). The apparatus must stretch discretionary capacity to absorb post-crisis demands, yet in doing so it weakens the claim to rule-bound expertise on which its depoliticized authority was constructed. The durability of the output-gap apparatus is therefore not evidence of its adequacy for successful macroeconomic management. It is evidence of institutional entrenchment: revision-prone, closure-dependent, and increasingly reliant on the discretionary repair its original architecture was designed to render unnecessary. That is the setting in which the capacity-utilization debates in political economy become historically intelligible.

## 2.3 Capacity-Utilization Debates in Political Economy

The durability of the output-gap apparatus makes the capacity-utilization debates in political economy historically intelligible, but it does not make the political-economy alternative straightforward. If output-gap estimates are revision-prone, closure-dependent, and institutionally entrenched, the question is what kind of measurement object political economy can place against them. The answer is less reassuring than the critique might suggest. Klär (2013) shows how unreliable potential-output and non-accelerating inflation rate of unemployment estimates constrained fiscal policy in Europe, producing contractionary impulses in economies operating below capacity. From a classical-Keynesian perspective, Palumbo (2015) gives the theoretical restatement: potential output is endogenous and path-dependent rather than an exogenous supply ceiling. The apparatus is therefore not merely imprecise; it is conceptually misconceived as a policy instrument. Mismeasurement, in this register, is not a technical nuisance. It allocates the costs of underutilization to workers and public budgets while insulating financial creditors from adjustment. The political-economy problem is therefore not only how to criticize the output gap, but what alternative empirical object can carry the theoretical claims made against it.

That question reveals an unresolved problem inside political economy itself. Across both sides of the capacity-utilization debate, the standard practice has often been to take the Federal Reserve Board series as an empirical object at face value. But that series is not a neutral measure waiting to be interpreted. It is a sedimented operational routine: a survey-centered instrument built for macroeconomic monitoring, stabilized inside the Federal Reserve’s statistical apparatus, and oriented toward the surveillance of manufacturing slack for policy purposes. Its transfer into political-economy research happened without a sustained examination of whether an instrument designed for governance dashboards could adjudicate theoretical claims about the long-run dynamics of capitalist accumulation.

The problem is not that the Federal Reserve Board series is useless. It has official authority, long historical coverage, sectoral detail, and continuity across time. Those properties make it valuable for monitoring. They do not automatically make it adequate for settling the theoretical question at stake in the utilization controversy. The series entered political-economy research through a combination of availability, continuity, and institutional credibility. But those are properties of an operational routine sedimented in institutions, not proof that the series can identify whether actual utilization converges toward a normal rate or whether the normal rate itself is shaped by demand history. The instrument was recruited from an institutional monitoring setting into a theoretical controversy without the conditions of that transfer being examined.

The historical timing of this transfer intensified the problem. The debate over the normal rate of capacity utilization gained contemporary momentum after the Global Financial Crisis, when debates within political economy gained renewed traction because the dominant macroeconomic apparatus had visibly failed. Yet the same period also deepened the institutional authority of the output-gap apparatus. Mainstream institutions were embedding output-gap estimates into governance infrastructure, while political-economy researchers were recruiting capacity-utilization series as scientific evidence for opposed theoretical claims. The same institutionalized measure therefore served two functions at once: governance in one setting, theoretical adjudication in another. A further scope constraint, developed more fully below, operated silently throughout: the Federal Reserve Board series is an industrial manufacturing survey. In the Fordist era, manufacturing covered a dominant sector of accumulation; in the post-Fordist economy, it traces a contracting share of output, employment, and investment while being used to characterize economy-wide capacity dynamics (Haluska, 2023). The representational problem deepened across the very period in which the series became most heavily used in political-economy research.

The opposition between the mainstream apparatus and political economy is therefore not merely a disagreement over indicators. It rests on incompatible understandings of cyclical dynamics. The output-gap apparatus hard-wires a New Keynesian Phillips curve into its estimation, treating business cycles as inflation cycles governed by the distance between unemployment and a non-accelerating inflation rate. That relationship bears little resemblance to Phillips’s original empirical finding, which identified an inverse relation between unemployment and the rate of change of money wages, not inflation (Phillips, 1958). Political economy builds the cycle on different foundations. In the models formalized by Goodwin—drawing on Phillips’s wage-employment regularity and Marx’s reserve-army mechanism—cycles are cycles of distributive conflict: wage share and employment oscillate around a trajectory shaped by accumulation dynamics rather than by deviations from an equilibrium inflation rate (Goodwin, 1967; Marx, 1999). What the mainstream apparatus measures as a gap from potential, political economy reads as a moment in a distributional struggle whose long-run trajectory is itself at stake. The controversy is therefore not only about whether utilization converges or drifts. It is about whether the cyclical object is an inflation phenomenon or a distributive one.

That difference gives the controversy its theoretical stakes. The Neo-Kaleckian tradition argues that the normal rate of capacity utilization is endogenous to demand. Firms revise desired utilization in response to persistent demand conditions through gradual adjustment of expectations (Lavoie, 1995), through micro-founded shift-choice mechanisms in which indivisibility makes utilization respond when capital cannot be smoothly resized (Nikiforos, 2013), or through hysteresis, where the history of demand shocks durably reshapes the utilization target itself (Setterfield & David-Avritzer, 2020). On this view, actual utilization need not converge to a structurally given benchmark. Persistent demand conditions can reshape long-run accumulation trajectories, and distributive outcomes become path-dependent.

The Sraffian-Keynesian tradition advances the opposed claim. In the classical formulation, the normal rate is determined by cost structures and competitive conditions, and actual utilization gravitates toward it over the long run, with the speed of convergence mediated by sluggish investment adjustment and the maintenance of desired reserve capacity under demand uncertainty (Ciccone, 1986). In the contemporary Sraffian-supermultiplier framework, autonomous demand drives the growth trend of output, while investment adjusts to bring capacity in line with demand; utilization therefore converges toward a normal rate anchored in distributionally and institutionally determined structural conditions, rather than in path-dependent revisions of desired utilization (Girardi & Pariboni, 2016, 2020; Serrano, 1995). Girardi and Pariboni (2019) press this critique against the Neo-Kaleckian side, arguing that Nikiforos’s micro-founded mechanism for endogenous normal utilization does not survive scrutiny: the cost-function specifications are internally inconsistent, the aggregation from micro to macro involves an unjustified logical leap, and the model breaks down once autonomous demand components are introduced.

The supermultiplier framework faces its own difficulty. Nikiforos (2018) argues that it makes investment quasi-endogenous, passively adjusting to the growth rate of autonomous demand, while the autonomy of so-called autonomous expenditure is itself untenable in the long run. From a stock-flow consistent standpoint, sustained growth of debt-financed autonomous spending implies either indefinitely rising liability-to-income ratios or an endogenous acceleration of accumulation that would stabilize them, leaving little room for the financial-fragility dynamics the post-Keynesian tradition otherwise treats as central. Cross-country evidence has been used to support the convergence claim: Gahn and González (2022) document stationarity of utilization across twenty-one countries and find a positive short-run but negligible long-run correlation between utilization and growth, a pattern they read as more compatible with convergence than with permanent Neo-Kaleckian drift.

The policy implications are direct, but they cannot be reduced to whether demand matters in the long run. In the Sraffian-Keynesian and supermultiplier perspective, demand remains central because autonomous demand governs the growth path while investment adjusts capacity so that utilization gravitates toward normal. In the Neo-Kaleckian view, the stronger claim is that persistent demand conditions can also reshape the normal rate itself, leaving durable traces in investment, employment, distribution, and accumulation trajectories. Haluska, Summa, and Serrano (2023) sharpen the Sraffian response through the logic of the traverse: actual utilization can remain away from normal for long periods after repeated demand-growth slowdowns without implying that the normal rate itself has become endogenous. The debate therefore cannot be resolved by presenting the two positions as parallel theoretical alternatives. It turns on whether the empirical instrument can carry information about the long-run relation between actual utilization, normal utilization, demand, and capacity adjustment. The inherited-instrument problem therefore returns with sharper force: the issue is not only whether the Federal Reserve Board series is the right proxy, but whether any single series, taken alone, can identify the structural relation at stake.

The construction of the Federal Reserve Board series makes that problem explicit. The stated design objective of the series ensures that the resulting measure exhibits mean reversion by construction and is incapable of registering chronic departure from normal capacity utilization. Testing whether the Federal Reserve Board series is stationary is therefore not a test of whether actual utilization converges to a normal rate fixed by cost conditions. It is a test of whether the Federal Reserve’s construction procedure successfully implemented its own design objective (Nikiforos, 2016). The difficulty is intensified by the opacity of that procedure: the raw micro-data are not publicly available, the weighting procedures combining different survey sources are not fully disclosed, and the splicing across institutional transitions lacks transparent documentation. The instrument cannot be subjected to independent methodological scrutiny by the researchers who rely on it (Shaikh, 2016). Nikiforos (2016, 2021b) develops this critique within the utilization controversy, arguing that the series is closer to a cyclical slack indicator than to a measure of long-run movements in normal utilization.

The Neo-Kaleckian response therefore turns to physical proxies. The average workweek of capital, associated with Foss’s work on the long-run workweek of fixed capital and revived by Nikiforos (2016, 2021b), alongside the national emergency utilization rate, avoids some of the limitations built into the Federal Reserve Board’s survey design by measuring machinery use, engineering capacity, or production speed rather than eliciting managerial assessments of desired operating rates (Foss, 1981, 1985; Nikiforos, 2016, 2021b). These proxies create a sharper contrast with the official series. They trend where the Federal Reserve Board series does not, and their divergence is read as evidence that the official series’ stationarity reflects measurement design rather than true convergence. This is the strongest empirical moment in the Neo-Kaleckian critique. But it does not close the identification problem. It remains unclear whether the proxy’s non-stationarity is evidence for endogenous normal utilization or simply a property of a different measurement object.

The Sraffian-convergence response presses precisely this limit. Gahn and González (2020) argue that the autoregressive distributed lag evidence does not robustly establish that the average workweek of capital delivers the stochastic-trend story required by the Neo-Kaleckian interpretation. Nikiforos (2020) replies that this does not rescue the Federal Reserve Board series, because the relevant question is not whether the alternative proxy resembles the official series, but whether the official series is appropriate for the theoretical object at stake. The exchange clarifies the impasse: the controversy is not only about which series is statistically preferable. It is about whether any single-series proxy strategy can identify a structural relation jointly shaped by utilization, demand, accumulation, and the formation of productive capacities.

Haluska (2023) extends the critique to measurement scope. The Federal Reserve Board series measures industrial utilization, not whole-economy utilization. A series designed around industrial productive capacity may be useful for monitoring manufacturing slack, but it cannot be transferred without mediation into a whole-economy controversy over accumulation, demand, and normal utilization. The problem is not simply that one proxy is bad and another is better. Different proxies change the measurement object. They do not, by themselves, identify the structural relation at stake.

What the debate has established is not which tradition is right, but that the instruments available to either side cannot settle the question. Both sides remain confined to single-series proxy disputes and single-equation identification strategies not well suited to recover the structural object at stake. If the long-run relationship between actual utilization and its normal benchmark is shaped by the joint dynamics of accumulation, demand, distribution, and technical change, it is better understood as a system property than as a property of any single observable series. The insufficiency is not one of data alone but of identification strategy.

This is the setting in which Shaikh’s critique becomes legible. Rather than adjudicate between proxies or defend an existing series, Shaikh’s treatment of capacity utilization opens a different route: identifying the structural relation governing capacity utilization through the accounting relation between actual and maximum profit rates at the normal cost of capital. The next section turns to that route, and to the question of whether it offers a structural identification of capacity utilization beyond the survey-centered and proxy-based commitments that have organized its measurement.

## 2.4 Shaikh’s Structural Identification of Capacity Utilization

Section 2.3 showed that the capacity-utilization controversy cannot be settled by choosing among survey measures and physical proxies. Shaikh enters this problem from a different angle. His aim is to recover a measure consistent with classical gravitation: capitalist economies move through disequilibrium paths organized around normal centers. His empirical route identifies that center through a cointegrating relation between output and capital, from which capacity utilization is constructed as the deviation of actual output from the implied capacity path.

This route shifts the measurement problem into profitability accounting. Shaikh does not treat capacity utilization as an independently observed series. He reconstructs the capital stock through a generalized perpetual inventory method and then uses the long-run relation between output and capital to define the path of productive capacity. Within that framework, utilization appears as the deviation of actual output from the capacity path implied by the estimated output–capital relation. The contribution is therefore not a better proxy, but a different form of identification: utilization is inferred through the accounting relation linking output, capital, and profitability.

The critical replication developed here begins from that contribution but shifts the object of scrutiny. In Shaikh’s presentation, the constructed utilization path carries much of the explanatory burden. This chapter instead asks what is being assumed by the coefficient that generates the capacity path in the first place. Once interpreted through unbalanced growth, that coefficient is not merely an econometric parameter. It is a candidate transformation elasticity linking capital accumulation to the formation of productive capacities.

Shaikh’s single-equation strategy treats the coefficient as fixed across the sample. If the coefficient only indexed a stable technical regularity, that restriction would be less consequential. But if it represents the transformation of accumulation into productive capacity, then the key question is whether that coefficient is robustly identified. Its stability matters, but so do uniqueness, admissibility, and sensitivity to the specification space. The critical replication therefore asks whether the coefficient remains stable, unique, and admissible once the relation is examined as a system rather than as a single equation.
