# Abstract and Introduction Rebuild Brief

The next editing move should be limited to the abstract and introduction.

The current abstract contains the whole chapter too densely: latent capacity, Shaikh, theta, S0, S1, S2, ARDL, VECM, omitted variable bias, exploitation, shock vectors, and the final political-economy conclusion all appear before the reader has entered the paper.

The introduction repeats much of that structure. This is why the chapter reads over-engineered early on. The task is not to delete the architecture. The task is to sequence it.

## Abstract target

The abstract should have five sentences.

1. Capacity utilization requires an unobserved productive-capacity benchmark.
2. Shaikh constructs this benchmark from a long-run output-capital relation.
3. This chapter critically replicates that strategy by interpreting the output-capital coefficient as a candidate transformation elasticity, theta.
4. The replication finds approximate recoverability, single-equation non-uniqueness, and system-level non-self-sufficiency of the bivariate relation.
5. The implication is that capacity identification is conditional on specification choice, historical controls, and distributional structure.

## Avoid in the abstract

- full stage-by-stage mechanics;
- “proves”;
- “severe omitted variable bias”;
- “exclusively” unless the result is being stated very carefully;
- long clauses naming every estimator and dummy;
- over-claiming the theoretical conclusion.

## Introduction target

The introduction should answer three questions in order.

### 1. What is being replicated?

Shaikh’s method of constructing productive capacity from a long-run output-capital relation.

### 2. Why does the replication matter?

Because the coefficient that generates the productive-capacity path determines the level, variance, and mean-reversion properties of the recovered utilization series.

### 3. What is the chapter’s contribution?

It reinterprets the coefficient as theta and tests whether it is reproducible, unique, and system-stable.

## Possible opening paragraph skeleton

> Capacity utilization is simple to state but difficult to measure. Actual output is observed; productive capacity is not. Any utilization index therefore depends on the construction of an unobserved productive ceiling. Shaikh’s capacity-utilization measure offers a portable solution by deriving this ceiling from a long-run relation between output and capital. This chapter critically replicates that strategy and asks whether the coefficient that draws the capacity path can sustain the structural meaning assigned to it.

## Possible contribution paragraph skeleton

> The chapter’s contribution is to shift attention from the utilization residual to the parameter that constructs the denominator of the index. Interpreted as a transformation elasticity, theta measures how capital accumulation is translated into productive-capacity formation. The empirical question is therefore not only whether a Shaikh-like utilization series can be reconstructed, but whether the underlying coefficient is unique across admissible single-equation models and stable in a joint system.

## Possible results paragraph skeleton

> The results are mixed in a specific sense. The Shaikh-like path is approximately recoverable, but exact reconstruction depends on data and lag choices that are not fully transparent. Once the ARDL model is opened to a wider specification grid, the admissible estimates form a disciplined but non-unique parameter space. At the system level, the bivariate output-capital relation does not survive as self-sufficient under the tested VECM design, while a restricted trivariate system survives when logged exploitation and historical shock controls enter the state vector.

## Possible closing paragraph skeleton

> These findings do not discard Shaikh’s strategy. They locate its boundary conditions. Capacity utilization can be measured through a portable output-capital framework, but the productive ceiling cannot be treated as a neutral technical object. Its empirical construction is conditioned by accumulation, distribution, and historically specific shocks.
