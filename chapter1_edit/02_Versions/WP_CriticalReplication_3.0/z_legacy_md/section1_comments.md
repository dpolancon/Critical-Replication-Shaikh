> Capacity utilization is simple to state but difficult to measure. Actual output reflects the level of effective demand, but does not directly reveal the degree of productive capacity in operation. For macroeconomic theory in the political economy tradition, this unobserved productive ceiling is a key variable linking growth and distribution: it determines the normal rate of profit, tracks the long-run trajectory of capital productivity and mechanization, defines the structural limits of economic expansion, and regulates the distributive conditions that shape the choice of technique and its underlying dynamics. \citet{Shaikh2016} offers a portable, accounting-based solution to this measurement problem by deriving the capacity ceiling from the long-run relation between output and capital using a cointegration methodology. This chapter develops a critical replication of that strategy, asking whether the empirical coefficient that draws the capacity path represents a stable structural law of production or merely a statistical artifact of omitted historical trends.

- [x] **Locked**

**Current Content:**
> This chapter examines Shaikh's capacity utilization measure by shifting the focus from the error-correction residuals—which proxy capacity utilization—to the estimated cointegrating coefficient itself. We interpret this parameter as a capacity transformation elasticity, $\theta$, which measures the percentage change in productive capacity generated by a one percent change in the capital stock. Standard post-Keynesian models typically assume a balanced-growth closure by constraining this elasticity to unity ($\theta = 1$). Under this restriction, Harrodian instability operates purely as a behavioral process: firms adjust investment in response to deviations of actual demand from normal capacity utilization. If $\theta \neq 1$, however, capacity formation is structurally decoupled from capital accumulation. An elasticity below unity ($\theta < 1$) characterizes an overaccumulation regime where capital growth outpaces capacity expansion, whereas an elasticity above unity ($\theta > 1$) leads to a regime of chronic excess capacity. By separating the transformation elasticity $\theta$ from Harrodian dynamics, we investigate whether this parameter remains stable across alternative single-equation specifications and whether the bivariate relationship between output and capital holds in a joint system.

> Re-estimating Shaikh's single-equation level relation using US data from 1947 to 2011 shows that the baseline point estimates are approximately recoverable, though exact reproduction is hindered by undocumented data and lag choices in the original study. Our replication reveals that small variations in these initial specifications generate substantial level shifts in the resulting utilization series. Evaluating a combinatoric grid of 500 Autoregressive Distributed Lag (ARDL) specifications incorporating variations of Shaikh's model, the estimates do not converge to a single benchmark or a narrow quantitative range of long-run coefficients. Instead, they trace a relatively wide parametric space where the recovered elasticity ranges from $0.65$ to $0.95$, depending on the researcher's choice of lag lengths, the inclusion of dummy variables for specific historical periods, and information-criterion penalties.

> This single-equation sensitivity necessitates testing the output--capital relation within a system framework. Our results show that when it includes only output and capital, a bivariate Vector Error Correction Model (VECM) fails cointegration and dynamic stability tests. The long-run level relationship holds only when we expand the state vector to a trivariate system where the logged rate of exploitation ($e_t$) enters the cointegrating vector, while historical dummy controls enter as deterministic controls in the VECM model. This is a central empirical finding of the chapter: rather than an insulated, technical relation, the output--capital multiplier requires explicit historical and distributional conditioning to establish a stable cointegrating relationship.

## Paragraph 1.5
- [x] **Locked**

**Current Content:**
> These findings do not invalidate Shaikh's framework; instead, they define its boundary conditions, meaning the limits within which the output--capital ratio can be treated as a stable anchor for capacity. We show that while capacity utilization can be measured using an output--capital anchor, the productive ceiling representing normal operating conditions cannot be treated as a neutral, engineering object. Rather, it is fundamentally a political-economic variable. Its empirical path must be conditioned by historically contingent controls and mediated by distributive variables such as the rate of exploitation. In other words, this chapter demonstrates that the long-run output--capital relation, interpreted as the capacity transformation elasticity $\theta$, cannot be estimated in isolation from the institutional and class conflicts that shape the capitalist labor process and technological change.
