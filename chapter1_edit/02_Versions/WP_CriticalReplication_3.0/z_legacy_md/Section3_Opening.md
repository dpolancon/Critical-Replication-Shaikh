## Paragraph 3.1 (Reconciled Draft)

> The conceptual framework of capacity utilization rests on a fundamental accounting challenge: while aggregate output is directly observed, the productive ceiling remains a latent, unobservable variable. This measurement problem is deeply entangled with the critique of aggregate production functions. As demonstrated by \citet{Shaikh1974} and elaborated by \citet{Felipe2005}, aggregate regressions using constant-price monetary data do not identify physical laws of production or marginal productivities, but instead approximate the underlying value-added accounting identity. Consequently, empirical fits of Cobb-Douglas or other production function specifications cannot validate neoclassical distribution theories. \citet{Shaikh2016} attempts to bypass these critiques by shifting from marginalist production functions to classical profit-rate accounting, deriving the capacity ceiling from the long-run cointegrating relation between aggregate output and capital. To ground this relationship descriptively in the US economy (1947–2011), we observe that while real GDP grew at an average annual rate of 3.2\% and the private capital stock grew at 3.0\%, the output-to-capital ratio remained stable in the long run but fluctuated around a historical average of 0.38. This relative stability suggests a close connection between capital accumulation and capacity expansion, but the persistent fluctuations confirm that the capacity ceiling is a dynamic economic variable rather than a fixed technical constant.

## Paragraph 3.2 (Reconciled Draft)

> To analyze the properties of the capacity ceiling, we specify the underlying production relation that maps capital accumulation into productive capacity. While \citet{Shaikh2016} derives this relation using profit-rate accounting, we ground his empirical output--capital relation in an aggregate Leontief production structure. As summarized by \citet{TavaniZamparelli2018}, the use of an aggregate Leontief production function with fixed labor-capital proportions is a standard practice in heterodox growth and distribution modeling. This framework treats capacity utilization not as a technical constant, but as a variable output-capital ratio that deviates from its normal ceiling.\footnote{Although we treat the capacity transformation elasticity, $\theta$, as a technological parameter in this chapter, subsequent chapters will endogenize $\theta$ to show how distributive struggles and changes in the wage share shape the long-run capacity ceiling.} To make the dynamics of this Leontief relation pedagogical, consider a stylized example where the capital stock grows at $g_k = 3\%$. If the capacity transformation elasticity is $\theta = 0.8$, the productive capacity ceiling grows at $g_{yp} = \theta g_k = 2.4\%$. Because capital accumulation outpaces capacity formation, the normal output-capital ratio falls, indicating a structural overaccumulation regime. We define these regime classifications under a single-sector macroeconomic bounding; while this simplifies the analysis of stagnation tendencies ($\theta > 1$) and overaccumulation ($\theta < 1$), we acknowledge this single-sector assumption as an analytical limitation that abstracts from multi-sector disproportionalities. The following subsection details this Leontief representation, outlining how we isolate the unobserved capacity path and estimate the long-run output--capital elasticity.

## Paragraph 3.3 (Reconciled Draft)

> Transitioning from this Leontief foundation to Shaikh's (2016) empirical design reveals a central econometric tension. In his capacity formulation, Shaikh does not treat the output--capital ratio as a constant engineering parameter; instead, he models the normal capacity-capital ratio as a function of both a deterministic time trend ($t$) representing autonomous technological change and capital-embodied technological change ($K$). Under this specification, however, the capacity transformation elasticity, $\theta$, remains a time-invariant technological parameter. By isolating $\theta$ from distributive struggle, this formulation ignores how conflict over the wage share mediates capacity formation, a key determinant of macroeconomic closure in heterodox theory \citep{Nikiforos2021closure}. Statistically, this isolation leads to a double misspecification. As \citet{Basu2020} demonstrates, excluding a relevant variable—in this case, distributive conflict and its influence on the choice of technique \citep{Kurz1986}—while including an irrelevant proxy like a deterministic time trend, introduces compound bias that contaminates the estimated coefficient on capital. Without these distributional variables, the estimated elasticity $\theta$ acts as a passive fitted slope that may simply capture an accounting identity rather than a stable law of production.
