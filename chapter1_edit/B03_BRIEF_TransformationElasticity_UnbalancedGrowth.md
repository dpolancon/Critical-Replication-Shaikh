# 3. From Shaikh’s Output–Capital Relation to the Transformation Elasticity

Actual output is observed. Productive capacity is not. Shaikh estimates productive capacity from a long-run relation between output and capital, then constructs capacity utilization as the deviation of observed output from that estimated benchmark. The residual utilization path is therefore downstream of the coefficient that generates the capacity path.

That coefficient is the object of Section 3. Interpreted through the surplus approach to political economy, the long-run output–capital coefficient is not a generic elasticity of output with respect to capital. It is a candidate transformation elasticity, θ: the parameter that measures how capital accumulation becomes productive capacity under unbalanced growth.

The theoretical benchmark comes from reproduction. Smooth reproduction requires that output, capital, and productive capacity move together under strict proportional conditions. In the limiting balanced-growth case, θ = 1: capital and productive capacity expand one-for-one. But capitalist accumulation need not satisfy that condition. Once θ differs from one, accumulation becomes structurally unbalanced.

The regime classification is simple:

| Regime | Condition | Capital/productive-capacity relation | Accumulation tendency |
|---|---:|---|---|
| Balanced growth | θ = 1 | Capital and productive capacity expand proportionally | Knife-edge benchmark |
| Overaccumulation / excess-capital tendency | θ < 1 | Capital grows faster than the productive capacity it generates | Accumulation decelerates toward stagnation under the pure closure |
| Excess productive-capacity tendency | θ > 1 | Productive capacity grows faster than capital | Accumulation accelerates without an internal supply-side brake under the pure closure |

This divergence is not triggered by Harrodian instability. It does not begin from a mismatch between planned saving and investment, or from demand failing to validate the warranted growth path. It arises earlier, inside the capital-to-capacity mapping itself. Under unbalanced growth, the supply-side accounting structure already generates divergent tendencies before the demand side, utilization, labor markets, and distributional conflict re-enter as stabilizing mediations.

This point gives Shaikh’s output–capital equation its theoretical force. His cointegrating relation estimates a capacity benchmark without relying on the Federal Reserve Board utilization series or on output-gap filters. The empirical logic is:

1. observe output and capital;
2. estimate the long-run output–capital relation;
3. interpret the long-run component as productive capacity;
4. construct utilization as the deviation of actual output from that estimated capacity path.

$$
