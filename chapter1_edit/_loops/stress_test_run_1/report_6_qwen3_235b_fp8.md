### **Corpus Baseline Profile (Diagnostic Dimensions)**

1. **Sentence-length variance / burstiness**  
   Human baseline: High variance. Mix of short, declarative statements ("This assumption fails empirically.") with long, nested sentences embedding econometric nuance. Frequent use of em-dashes and parentheses for asides. Burstiness index (qualitative): high.

2. **Paragraph rhythm**  
   Paragraphs vary in function: some are dense with citations and technical claims, others are interpretive or narrative. No rigid formula. Some end with a punchy conclusion; others trail into open-ended reflection. Repetitive paragraph shapes are rare.

3. **Connective/transition tics**  
   Low reliance on "moreover," "furthermore," "it is important to note." Instead, transitions are often implicit or grounded in logical sequencing ("If this holds, then…", "Yet the data show…"). Triadic lists are rare unless rhetorically motivated.

4. **Hedging density and placement**  
   Hedging is concentrated around contested claims or data limitations ("may suggest", "appears inconsistent with"). No hedging around core methodological critiques. Hedging is not uniformly distributed.

5. **Citation integration**  
   Citations are woven into syntax: e.g., "As Jayadev (2006) argues, liberalization did not reduce inequality, but rather restructured it." Rarely tacked on at clause end.

6. **Local terminological dialect**  
   Strong preference for heterodox PE terms: *accumulation regime*, *distributional conflict*, *class struggle*, *historical specificity*, *closure*, *endogenous technical change*. Avoids textbook terms like "efficiency," "equilibrium," "optimization" unless critically framed.

7. **Imperfection signature**  
   Drafts show uneven polish: some sections tightly argued, others contain rough edges (e.g., "We leave this for future work" without elaboration). Occasional idiosyncratic phrasing ("the numbers don’t lie, but they do stutter").

8. **Specificity of illustrative material**  
   Empirical references are institutionally grounded: e.g., "the 1983 Fed policy shift," "Korean War demobilization," "IMF conditionality in Indonesia." Avoids generic "in many countries" or "recent trends."
